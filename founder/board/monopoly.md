# Monopoly lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **A small market you could own is named.** 150 schools across four zones in Hyderabad and Secunderabad is the right kind of starting market for this lens: small, bounded and close enough to dominate before expanding. Most edtech ideas start with "all students"; this one starts with a list.
- **Distribution is designed, not assumed.** The school-led B2B2C journey (outreach, demo, pilot, orientation, diagnostics, activation, review, conversion) is a real channel plan. Selling to a school brings in a group of students in one deal instead of buying them one at a time.
- **Each founder owns a zone.** As described, the team structure puts a named owner on each piece of the channel. That matters more here than any feature.
- **The product exists.** A working build means the next test is distribution, not engineering.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **Nothing stops a copycat in month six.** The core runs on widely available AI APIs: adaptive flows, AI explanations and text-to-speech can all be rebuilt by anyone with the same APIs. Nothing in the brief is roughly ten times better than the next option, and none of the four durable advantages (proprietary technology, network effects, economies of scale, brand) exists yet. If the moat is "we built it first", it is not a moat.
2. **The channel is unproven and may cost too much.** Zero signed pilots, no conversion rates and no school revenue share are known. A sales cycle that runs from meetings to proposals to pilots to orientation costs a lot per school in founder time. If four founders can close only a handful of schools a term, and the school takes a large share of ₹299, the distribution maths may not work at any scale.
3. **A crowded buyer.** Schools and parents already pay for alternatives: tuition centres, other edtech apps, and general-purpose AI tools students can use for free. Who else sells into these same 150 schools is an open question for /founder-competitors. If the answer is "many", this is a fight over thin margins, which is exactly what this lens warns against.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- What is the secret: what do you know about Hyderabad schools or how these students learn that others do not?
- If a well-funded competitor copied every feature in six months, what would you still have that they could not get?
- Which durable advantage are you building on purpose? Could school-level learning data, curriculum-aligned diagnostics or relationships that lock schools in become one?
- How do the first 10 schools arrive, how many founder-hours does each one take, and what is left of ₹299 after the school's share and API costs?
- Why now? What has changed that makes schools ready to adopt this this year?
- What share of the 150 schools must you hold before expanding, and how will you know you dominate the zone?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Prove the channel on a small sample.** Signed, structured pilots with a small set of schools from the 150, with time-to-close and founder-hours per school recorded, before any expansion beyond the four zones.
2. **Name the moat in writing.** A specific plan for one durable advantage that does not depend on the AI APIs (for example proprietary diagnostic data across a school's students, curriculum alignment, or school-side switching costs), with a first measurable sign of it in the pilots.
3. **Show unit economics per school.** The school revenue share and the AI/API cost per student, measured in the pilots, showing a positive margin per paid user at ₹299.
4. **Map the competition.** A /founder-competitors pass on who else already sells into these schools and parents, and why a school would pick Edurance over them.

If the pilots cannot show conditions 1 and 3, this lens would move to PASS.
