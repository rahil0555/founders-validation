# Product lens
Score: 4 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- There is a working product, not a deck. Diagnostics with saved results, lesson resumption, adaptive flows, explanations and narration with audio caching exist, according to the founders. Caching audio and resuming lessons are the kind of unseen craft this lens values.
- Selling through schools is sensible. A school can put a whole class in front of the product at once, which makes the product easier to test properly.
- The core promise can be said in one sentence: "find exactly where each student is stuck and teach that gap next." The product has not yet been narrowed to it.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **No one thing.** The brief lists diagnostics, adaptive paths, AI explanations, voice, mastery tracking, parent features, school visibility and a long-term AI tutor. That is several products. A four-person team spread across all of them will do each one adequately and none brilliantly. Students drop products that are merely adequate. The pitch still needs a paragraph, which tells this lens the product is not yet clear.
2. **The first five minutes is a test.** The planned journey puts baseline diagnostics first. For a student, opening a new app to sit another exam is the opposite of a good first experience, and nothing in the brief says what the student gets back right away. If the first session feels like homework with nothing in return, the "will students keep using it" question gets answered no in week one.
3. **The seams between five parties.** The plan runs school decision-maker → teacher orientation → student login → parent visibility → paid conversion. The founders handle each handover by hand in each zone. Each one can break. Teachers may not see why it matters to them. Logins may fail on shared or home devices. Parents may get reports they never asked for. The person asked to pay ₹299 may never have seen the product working. Nobody in the brief owns the whole journey from start to finish. It is also unclear who pays, the parent or the school, and the experience should be designed backwards from that person.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- In one sentence, what does Edurance do better than anything else, and which features would you switch off tomorrow if you had to?
- Walk me through a student's first five minutes, screen by screen. What do they get back before the diagnostic is over?
- Which subject and grade is the product best at today? Why not launch with only that?
- Who is the paying customer for ₹299, and what have they actually seen of the product by the time they are asked to pay?
- What does the teacher do in a normal week? If the answer is "nothing", who keeps the students coming back?
- Where has a pilot handover already gone wrong (login, device, language, network), and what did you change?
- How do you check that AI explanations are correct and fit the curriculum before a student sees them?
- What does each active student cost to serve in AI and text-to-speech calls? (Not yet known.)

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Cut to one thing.** Choose one subject, one grade band and one core loop (find the gap, teach it, show the student it is closed). Hide or freeze everything else, including the broader parent and school dashboards, until that loop holds up.
2. **Redesign the first session.** A student should finish a first lesson they want to come back to within the first few minutes. Cut the diagnostic down or spread it through the lessons, rather than putting a full test up front.
3. **Map and own every seam.** Write down every handover from the school signing to a parent paying, and name the one founder responsible for each. Run one or two small pilots and record where each handover broke.
4. **Name the payer and measure honestly.** Decide whether the parent or the school pays. Then report student activation and repeat use from the pilots as observed numbers, before any expansion across the 150-school map.
